So what is AI?

"The study is to proceed on the basis of the conjecture that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it."

Dartmouth summer research proposal, 1956. The paper in which the term "artificial intelligence" was coined. jmc.stanford.edu

Artificial Intelligence is the simulation of intelligence, especially learning.

Nothing about robots, nothing about chips, nothing about ChatGPT. A machine that behaves as if it were intelligent. Whether it "really" is intelligent is a philosophy seminar, not this one.

Two minutes. Read the quote, then click: the course definition, this one is in the exam. Point out that "simulation" is doing a lot of work: we do not claim the machine understands anything, we claim it behaves as if. The 1956 people thought a summer would do it. It took 70 years and, as we will see, a few trillion dollars.

Generative or general?

Same first syllable, very different claim. The press mixes them up daily.

Narrow AI

one job
  • fraud yes/no, fastest route, face unlock
  • brilliant at its task, useless outside it
  • everything built from 1956 to 2021

Generative AI

creates
  • produces text, images, code, audio
  • one model, thousands of tasks: broad, not general
  • still a machine learner: it learned from examples
  • 2022 (ChatGPT) to today

General AI (AGI)

anything
  • any intellectual task a human can do, as well as a human
  • does not exist (2026)
  • the bet behind the money on the next slides
Generative is not general. ChatGPT writes your cover letter and still cannot run ShopNow. Whether the second follows from the first is the open question of the decade.

2. Seventy years in three acts

Act 1 (1956–1990s): write the rules. Logic, search, expert systems. Two "AI winters": the promises were bigger than the computers, funding was cut.
Act 2 (1990s–2012): learn from data. Statistics meets computing. Deep Blue 1997, spam filters, Netflix. Small data, small models.
Act 3 (2012–): deep learning, then generative. AlexNet 2012, AlphaGo 2016, transformers 2017, ChatGPT 2022. The grey half of the picture.

Image: Wikimedia Commons, AI-History-Timeline. Self-study for the exam: the individual milestones. Exam: "Era 1 (pre-deep learning) focused on rules, logic, expert systems".

Three minutes, use the picture as a map, do not read every box. Act 1: people wrote the intelligence down by hand, it worked for chess and for 200 rules, not for 2 million. Winters: 1974 and 1987, the money left because the promises were too big. Act 2: let the machine find the rules, works as soon as you have data. Act 3: neural networks become deep, and from 2022 they generate. The timeline's own grey/blue split is exactly our morning/afternoon split. Then: "What changed in 2012? Three things."

Data

Compute

×2 / 2 yr

Algorithms

Three ways to learn

Supervised

with an answer key
  • data: inputs x plus the right answer y (the label)
  • goal: predict y for new x
  • output a number: regression (delivery time, demand)
  • output a category: classification (fraud yes/no, churn)

Unsupervised

no answer key
  • data: inputs x only, nobody labelled anything
  • goal: find structure
  • groups: clustering (customer segments)
  • the odd ones out: anomaly detection

Reinforcement

trial, error, reward
  • data: none at the start, the agent creates it by acting
  • goal: maximise reward over time
  • examples: game AI (AlphaGo), robots, dynamic pricing
  • and: how ChatGPT learned manners (afternoon)
Ask one question of any AI project: is there a label? Yes: supervised. No: unsupervised. Nobody knows the right answer until we act: reinforcement.
Three minutes, this is the slide that structures the pitch. Supervised: like learning with the solutions at the back of the book. Unsupervised: someone dumps a box of photos on your desk and says "sort them". Reinforcement: training a dog, nobody shows it the trick, it gets a treat when it gets closer. The one question at the bottom is the tool the groups need in a minute: is there a label?

4. Supervised: fit the line (live)

ShopNow deliveries: distance to the customer (x) and delivery time (y). Find the line. Then let the machine try.

Six minutes. Phase 1 (2 min): a student calls out w and b, you move the sliders, everyone watches the loss number. "Lower is better. Who beats 40?" Phase 2: press "1 step" three times and narrate: the machine computes in which direction w and b must move so the loss drops (the gradient), and takes a small step against it. Then "let the machine learn": it repeats until the loss stops improving. Phase 3: set the learning rate to the largest value and run again: it explodes. Step size matters. Tick "show best line": the machine found it, no human wrote a formula for delivery times. Everything on the next slide is in the exam.

What the machine just did: training

1. Predict

\( \hat{y} = w \cdot x + b \)
  • the model is a line with two parameters
  • start with random w and b

2. Measure the error: loss

\( L = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2 \)
  • mean squared error (MSE): the average squared distance between line and points
  • one number, lower is better

3. Update: gradient descent

\( w \leftarrow w - \eta \, \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} \)
  • the gradient says in which direction the loss grows
  • move opposite to it, by a step of size η: the learning rate

4. Repeat

until L stops improving
  • each round: predict, loss, update
  • too large a step: it explodes, too small: it takes forever
This recipe, predict → loss → update → repeat, is all of machine learning. A neural network has millions of w's instead of one. ChatGPT has a trillion. Same loop.
Two minutes, four exam facts: MSE is the loss for regression, gradient descent moves opposite the gradient, the learning rate is the step size, training stops when the loss stops improving. The takeaway is the bridge to 10:40: the NN deck starts exactly here, with this line, and adds more parameters.

Same recipe, different output: classification

Regression outputs a number. Classification outputs a category, usually via a probability.

Training is the same loop: predict, loss, update, repeat. Only the loss changes (not MSE, but "how surprised was the model by the true label").

The label comes from the past: an order is "fraud" if a chargeback arrived within 90 days. No chargeback history, no model.

Homework Part B was exactly this on ShopNow data. At 11:30 your groups compete on it: the leaderboard.

Two minutes. Exam: regression outputs a number, classification outputs a category label. The picture is the fraud example from the game, now as a trained model. Point at the label definition: this is where business knowledge enters, a data scientist cannot define "fraud" alone. Bridge to the leaderboard at 11:30.

5. Unsupervised: find the groups (live)

ShopNow customers: average basket (x) and orders per month (y). No label anywhere. How many groups do you see?

Six minutes. Phase 1 (1 min): nothing is coloured. "How many groups? Point at them." Most say three. Phase 2: press the three buttons in order, twice, narrating: centroids are dropped at random, every point joins the nearest centroid, each centroid moves to the middle of its points. Repeat until nothing moves. Then "run to the end". Phase 3: set k = 2 and k = 5 and run again: the algorithm always delivers k groups, it never says "there are three". Choosing k is your job. Phase 4: "name the groups": the algorithm only gives numbers 0, 1, 2. Value seekers, loyalists, premium are names humans add afterwards. Exam: initialise, assign, update, repeat; output is a cluster number per point.

k-means in four moves

1. Initialise

  • you choose k
  • drop k centroids at random

2. Assign

  • every point joins its nearest centroid
  • that is the only "decision"

3. Update

  • move each centroid to the mean of its points
  • hence the name

4. Repeat

  • until the centroids stop moving
  • usually under 20 rounds
Output: a cluster number for every customer, nothing else. The algorithm does not know what "premium" means. It also does not know k. Both come from you, and both go into a BI dashboard afterwards.
One minute, the four moves are an exam question. Then the honest limitations: k is a human choice, names are a human choice, and the result depends on the random start (run it twice, sometimes it differs). Bridge to BI: segments end up as a column in the warehouse and a filter in the dashboard.

6. Reinforcement: learning by doing

No answer key, no fixed dataset. An agent acts, the environment answers with a new state and a reward. The agent updates its policy: what to do in which state.

ShopNow: which discount to show this visitor? Reward: margin of the order. After a million visitors the policy knows more than any marketing rule.

Famous: AlphaGo (2016) learned Go by playing itself. Boston Dynamics robots learn to walk in simulation first.

This afternoon: ChatGPT's last training step is reinforcement from human feedback: people rate answers, the rating is the reward.

Costly to run on real customers: every bad action costs money. Hence simulators, and small traffic first, like Saturday's test phase.

Two minutes, one slide. Exam: reinforcement learning learns by rewards and feedback. The ShopNow example links to the A/B test: an A/B test is a one-shot version of the same question, "which variant earns more", a reinforcement agent asks it continuously and shifts traffic as it learns (bandits). Then the last two slides.

7. Before you believe a model: three questions

Where did the data come from?

  • garbage in, garbage out: a model is only as good as Saturday's staging tables
  • fraud model trained on 2019 orders, deployed in 2026: fraud has moved
  • biased past, biased model: hiring data from a company that never hired women

What exactly is the label?

  • "churn" = no order in 90 days? 180? Cancelled account?
  • leakage: a column that only exists after the answer is known (refund amount for a return model)
  • the label is a business decision, not a data science one

How was it tested?

  • never on the data it learned from: hold out a test set (11:30: the leaderboard uses one)
  • offline accuracy is a promise, Saturday's A/B test is the proof
  • monitor it: models rot while the world moves on
Day 1, your lecturer's own line: "the AI model is the easy part. The hard part is the data it can reach." Everything you learned on Saturday is the hard part.
Two minutes. This is where the business student earns their money: the questions a non-programmer must ask. Data origin, label definition, test procedure. The quote is from the introduction deck on day 1, students have heard it. Leakage example: a return model that uses "refund amount" is cheating, the refund exists only after the return.

Takeaways

  • AI is the simulation of intelligence, especially learning. Symbolic AI: humans write the rules. Machine learning: the rules are learned from examples. Deep learning: with neural networks. Generative AI: the output is new content. Generative is not general.
  • Three acts: rules (1956–1990s, two winters), learning from data (1990s–2012), deep and generative (2012–). The ideas are old, data and compute made them work.
  • The money: private AI investment ×60 since 2013, 345 bn in 2025, half of it generative. Big Tech spends more on data centres in 2026 than the moon landing cost. Adoption faster than the PC or the internet.
  • Three ways to learn: supervised (labels: regression gives a number, classification a label), unsupervised (no labels: k-means gives a cluster number), reinforcement (rewards). First question of every project: is there a label?
  • Training = predict, loss (MSE), update opposite the gradient by the learning rate, repeat until the loss stops improving. Same loop from a line to ChatGPT.
  • Before you believe a model: where is the data from, what is the label, how was it tested.
