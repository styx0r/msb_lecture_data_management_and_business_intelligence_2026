The fix: flip a coin

Randomized experiment

  • every visitor is assigned at random to A (old) or B (new)
  • on average both groups are identical: same share of loyal users, mobile users, bargain hunters, ...
  • the only systematic difference is the checkout
  • so a difference in conversion (beyond noise) is caused by the checkout

Names for the same thing

A/B test online controlled experiment split test randomized controlled trial (medicine) bucket test
  • A = control, B = treatment / variant
  • the coin flip is the whole trick. Everything else today is about handling the noise.
Same idea as clinical trials: placebo vs drug. Randomization removes confounders on average, it does not remove noise. That is what statistics is for.

Who does this?

Everywhere online

  • Booking.com, Amazon, Netflix, Google, Microsoft, Spotify, Zalando, ...
  • thousands of experiments per year, often >1000 running at the same time
  • every button, ranking, price display, email subject line

Most ideas fail

  • Microsoft: only about 1/3 of tested ideas improved their target metric, 1/3 did nothing, 1/3 hurt
  • experts are bad at predicting which ideas win
  • that is the business case for testing

Cheap wins exist

  • Bing: moving part of the ad text into the headline, a few days of work
  • +12% revenue, one of the best ideas in Bing's history
  • it had sat in the backlog for months because nobody believed in it

Anatomy of an A/B test

Steps 1 to 3 happen before a single user sees variant B. Most A/B testing mistakes come from skipping them.

1. A good hypothesis

"Replacing the 3-page checkout by a 1-page checkout increases the checkout conversion rate from 5.0% by at least 0.5 percentage points, because fewer steps mean fewer drop-offs."

Contains

  • the change (one thing)
  • the metric and its current value
  • the smallest effect we care about
  • a reason (so we learn something even if it fails)

Not a hypothesis

  • "let's see if the new design does something"
  • "we redesigned checkout, navigation and pricing, let's test"
  • "we will pick whatever metric looks best afterwards"

2. Metrics

Role ShopNow checkout example Type
Primary metric (exactly one) checkout conversion rate: buyers / visitors of the checkout binary per user (0/1), compared as a proportion
Secondary metrics revenue per visitor, average basket, time to complete checkout continuous per user, compared as a mean
Guardrail metrics page load time, error rate, returns, support tickets, unsubscribes must not get worse, otherwise no ship

Why one primary metric?

  • decided before the test, so nobody can cherry-pick afterwards
  • more metrics = more chances of a false "win" (later: multiple testing)

Why revenue per user is harder

  • most users spend 0, a few spend a lot
  • next slide: what that does to the number you measure

One experiment, a thousand times

1. One experiment

  • two weeks, coin flip per visitor
  • A: 5,000 visitors, 250 buy = 5.00%
  • B: 5,000 visitors, 262 buy = 5.24%
  • lift = 5.24 − 5.00 = +0.24 pp
  • that is the whole result of one A/B test: one number

2. Rewind, run it again

  • same checkout, same two weeks, other visitors
  • A: 243 buy, B: 239 buy: lift −0.08 pp
  • and again: +0.31 pp, and again: −0.19 pp
  • nothing was changed, the number moves anyway
  • that movement is noise

3. 1,000 reruns = a histogram

  • the simulator reruns the experiment 1,000 times in a second
  • each bar counts how many of the 1,000 reruns ended with a lift in that small range
  • tall bar at 0: most reruns land near zero
  • orange part of a bar: the test said "significant" in those reruns
In real life you run the experiment once and never see the histogram. The next slides pick the number of users so that your single number behaves: at most 5% false alarms and a real effect found 80% of the time.
This is the bridge for beginners, do not skip it. Card 1: an experiment is one run of the test, two numbers, one difference. Make them compute the lift once. Card 2: the thought experiment of a time machine, same two weeks with different people. Ask: would the number be exactly the same? Why not? Card 3: the simulator is that time machine. Say explicitly: x-axis = the lift one rerun showed, bar height = how many reruns showed that lift. Takeaway: we only ever get one bar's worth of information in reality, so we design the test (number of users) such that the invisible histogram has the properties we want: 5% and 80% are design targets, not results. The enemy: noise (live)

Same checkout in both groups (an A/A test). What lift do we see anyway?

run
Key intuition for the whole lecture, so take 5 minutes here. Script: lift 0, 5,000 users. Click "1 experiment" a few times: every run shows a different lift, nothing was changed. The small marker shows where the last experiment landed, so one click = one count in one bar. Then +1000: the histogram. Read the range out loud: nothing changed, and still we saw anything between about -1.3 and +1.4 pp. Then back to the checkout: +3 pp is far outside the band, so it is not noise, but that only helps if the two groups were random (they were not, logged-in users only). Orange bars are p below 0.05, about 5% of them: that is alpha, before we even define it. If an experiment lands left of zero and someone asks what its p-value means: the test is two-sided, so p is the share of A/A experiments that land at least this far from zero on either side, left or right. Read it as "if nothing changed, noise alone produces a difference at least this big in 31% of experiments". Two-sided because B can also be worse than A, and we want to catch that too; the band is symmetric for the same reason. Now users to 50,000: the grey band and the histogram narrow. The band is the only "noise knob" there is: for yes/no data the spread is fixed by n and the baseline rate b, SE = sqrt(2 b (1-b) / n). Say where the band comes from in two sentences: each visitor is a coin with 5% heads, add up 5,000 coins and you get a bell curve whose width is known in advance (the dashed green line). The band is the middle 95% of that bell curve, computed before any experiment ran. The bars are the check: the reruns fill the curve. Then lift to +1 pp: the green line leaves the band, the orange share is the power. Every observed difference is effect + noise. Statistics quantifies how big noise alone can be.

The question, properly asked

Null hypothesis H0

  • "the new checkout changes nothing"
  • any difference we see is noise
  • the boring default we try to reject

Alternative H1

  • "the new checkout changes the conversion rate"
  • what we hope is true

If H0 were true, how often would chance alone
produce a difference at least as big as the one we saw?

That number is the p-value.

The p-value as a picture (live)

We run the experiment once and get one number. Even so, we can compute the bell curve for "B changes nothing" (H0) without any reruns. Then we ask: if H0 is true, how likely is a difference this big or bigger? That probability is p.

1. The H0 bell curve is known in advance (formula), the reruns only illustrate it.
2. p = probability under H0 of our difference or a bigger one, on either side.
3. p < α (5%, fixed before the test): statistically significant, we reject H0.
This slide only adds one thing to the noise slide: our real experiment, the orange line. Say it slowly: in reality we run the test once and get one number, +0.9 pp. The 1,000 blue reruns are the time machine where nothing changed. Red = the reruns that landed at least 0.9 pp away from zero, on either side (a big negative lift would surprise us just as much). Count them: 41 of 1,000, that is p. The formula gives the same number without rerunning anything. Alpha is the other thing on the slide: the band we fixed before the test, 5%. Orange bars on the noise slide were exactly the reruns outside this band. Script: slide to +0.5, p = 25%, inside the band, noise does this all the time. Slide to +3.0 (the checkout story): no rerun ever got there, p under 0.01%, which is why the dashboard number looked so convincing. The problem there was the groups, not the statistics. Then alpha to 1%: the band widens.

What a p-value is not

People sayReality
"p = 0.04, so there is a 96% chance B is better" no. p assumes H0 is true and asks how surprising the data is. It says nothing about the probability of H0 or H1.
"p = 0.3, so the new checkout has no effect" no. We just did not detect one. Maybe the effect is small, maybe we had too few users.
"p = 0.001, so the effect is huge" no. With millions of users even a +0.01 pp effect gets a tiny p-value. Significance says "real", not "relevant".
"p = 0.06, so close, let's run a few more days" that is peeking. Coming up.
Always report the effect size with a confidence interval next to the p-value (what that is: in the Analyze section).

Two ways to be wrong

B really has no effect B really has an effect
Test says "no effect" correct Type II error (missed it), probability β
Test says "effect!" Type I error (false alarm), probability α correct, probability = power = 1 - β

α = 5%: false alarm

  • we ship something useless (or harmful) and believe it works
  • smoke alarm goes off, no fire

β = 20%: missed effect

  • we throw away a good idea
  • fire, but the alarm stays quiet

power = 80%

  • if the effect is really there, we find it 4 out of 5 times
  • the usual convention, like α = 5%

α, β and power in one picture (live)

True lift 0: the p-value picture, the only world with false alarms (α). True lift above 0: the reruns move right, the ones left inside the band are missed effects (β). The band decides what we say, the world decides whether we were right.
Start with true lift 0: this is the p-value picture again, blue reruns, red ones outside the band are the false alarms, alpha. Then turn the true lift up to +1.0: the bars move right and turn green, this is the world where B really works. Grey = the reruns that still land inside the band, we would say "not significant" although B works: beta. The H0 curve stays as a dashed line with red tails: alpha is a property of the band, it does not change when the truth changes. Then the orange line, our one real experiment: at +0.7 inside the band. With true lift 0 that verdict is right, with true lift +1.0 it is a type II error. Slide it to +1.2: now "significant", a false alarm at true lift 0, right at +1.0. We never know which world we are in. Let a student drive: alpha to 10%: band narrower, less beta, more alpha. Users up: everything narrows, beta shrinks. This trade-off is why sample size matters, next section.

3. How many users do we need?

The order is fixed. The one real decision is the smallest lift worth shipping. α and power are conventions. Users and days are the result.

1. Baseline

5%
  • a fact, from the BI dashboard

2. MDE

+0.5 pp
  • the decision: minimum detectable effect, below this we would not ship

3. α, power

5%, 80%
  • conventions, the same in every test

4. Users, days

31,200
  • the result, not a knob: per group, a month at 2,000 visitors/day
Lots of traffic, big effect: the formula says days. Run it short, move on. Little traffic or a small effect: it says months. Then test a bigger change, or accept that effects this small are invisible to you. Lowering the power until the number fits is not planning, it is deciding the answer first.
The order matters: baseline is measured, the MDE is the one business decision, alpha and power are conventions, users and days fall out. Students (and product teams) tend to run it backwards: "we have two weeks, which parameters make two weeks enough?" Say why that is self-deception: power down to 60% means you miss a real effect in 40% of the cases and only feel like you planned; MDE up to +1 pp means you are no longer testing the question you had. The sample size is the number you get, not the number you want. The two practical cases: big shop and a bold change, the test is over in days, do not run it longer than needed. Small shop or a subtle change, the formula says months; then test bigger changes or be honest that this test cannot answer the question. The next slides give the formula, the numbers and the live planner.

The one formula of the day (for demonstration only)

\[ n_{\text{per group}} \;\approx\; \frac{16 \cdot b\,(1-b)}{\delta^{2}} \]

rule of thumb for α = 5%, power = 80%. b = baseline conversion rate (not the p-value, hence a different letter), δ = MDE, the minimum detectable effect in percentage points. The 16 hides the α and power choices.

Halve the effect, quadruple the users. That is the δ² in the denominator, and the reason small shops cannot test small changes.
This is Lehr's rule. Full version: n = (z_{1-α/2} + z_{1-β})² · 2·b(1-b) / δ². Textbooks write p for the baseline rate, we use b so it does not collide with the p-value. With z = 1.96 and 0.84 the squared sum is 7.85, times 2 is about 16. We will not derive it, the notebook uses statsmodels.

The tool: statsmodels


from statsmodels.stats.power import NormalIndPower
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportion_effectsize

baseline = 0.05          # 5.0% convert today
mde      = 0.005         # we care about +0.5 pp (5.0% -> 5.5%)
effect   = proportion_effectsize(baseline + mde, baseline)   # pp -> one number for statsmodels (Cohen's h), never interpreted

n_per_group = NormalIndPower().solve_power(
    effect_size=effect, alpha=0.05, power=0.8, alternative="two-sided"
)

print(f"{n_per_group:,.0f} users per group")
# 31,217 users per group
# at 2,000 checkout visitors per day: about 31 days
Why decide this before the test: (1) you know if the test is even feasible, (2) you know when to stop, so you are not tempted to peek.

4 + 5. Randomize and run

Do

  • randomize per user (not per session or page view), and keep a user in their group
  • run for whole weeks: Monday shoppers differ from Sunday shoppers
  • run until the planned sample size, then stop
  • check the split: 50/50 planned should be 50/50 observed

Don't

  • change variant B while the test runs ("just a small fix")
  • stop early because it looks good (or bad)
  • start on Black Friday
  • test on a subgroup only and then generalize
Sample ratio mismatch: planned 50/50, got 50.8% / 49.2% with 100,000 users? That is not noise (p < 0.001), that is a bug: a redirect that fails on old browsers, a bot filter, a caching layer. Do not analyze, fix first.

Peeking: the most common mistake

run
Looking every day and stopping at the first p < 0.05 turns the promised 5% false alarm rate into about 25-30%. The p-value is only valid for the sample size you planned.
Lift 0, so there is nothing to find. Click "1 test" a few times: the p-value random-walks, some paths dip below 0.05 and come back. Then +100 twice: right bars. Deciding once at the end gives the promised 5%, stopping at the first dip gives 25-30%. More days: it gets worse, not better. Looking at the dashboard is fine; deciding based on it is not. Peeking by design: sequential testing (outlook).

This is the moment after the test. Two numbers come back from the dashboard. Ask the room what they would report to the product team: most say "+0.72 pp". Then the three questions. The p-value answers only the first one, and by now they can read it: +0.72 is far outside the ±0.35 band for 31,000 users per group. The second is just subtraction. The third is the new thing: the same number could have been +0.5 or +0.9 in a rerun, and the business wants to know how far off we might be. That is the confidence interval.

The tool: three lines of statsmodels


from statsmodels.stats.proportion import proportions_ztest
from statsmodels.stats.proportion import confint_proportions_2indep

# after 31 days: visitors and buyers per group
visitors = [31_250, 31_180]     # A, B
buyers   = [ 1_560,  1_780]     # A, B

z, p = proportions_ztest(count=buyers, nobs=visitors)
# z = -3.98, p = 0.0001

# B first, so the interval is for B - A
lo, hi = confint_proportions_2indep(
    buyers[1], visitors[1], buyers[0], visitors[0]
)
# difference B - A = +0.72 pp, 95% CI [+0.36 pp, +1.07 pp]
Two-sample z-test for proportions. The chi-squared test on the 2x2 table gives the same answer. The t-test is the same idea for means (revenue per user): scipy.stats.ttest_ind.
Do not derive the test statistic. Say: it is the observed difference divided by the noise level from the A/A slide. z = 3 means "3 noise units away from zero".

Confidence interval: the band, moved (live)

So far the band sat around 0 and we asked "is our number inside?". Put the same band around our number instead: that is the 95% confidence interval. Read it as: we are 95% confident that the true lift lies somewhere in this interval. If it does not contain 0, the result is significant, and the interval tells the business how big the effect could be.
Nothing new is computed here. The band width is the noise level from the A/A slide, ±1.96 SE. What "95% confident" means precisely: if we reran the experiment many times and built this band around each result, 19 of 20 of those bands would contain the true lift. We get one of them and do not know whether it is one of the 19, we only know that misses are rare. Say it once, then use the plain reading on the slide. Top row: the band around 0, the p-value view. Bottom row: the same band around our observed +0.72, the interval view. Both say the same thing about significance (0 is outside the orange band exactly when +0.72 is outside the grey band), but the interval also says how big the effect could plausibly be: anywhere from +0.36 to +1.07, most likely near +0.72. Slide the observed lift to +0.3: the orange band now contains 0, not significant, and the business sees that the true effect could be zero or even negative. Users down to 5,000: both bands widen. statsmodels uses the observed rates for the width, which is why its interval differs in the second decimal.

Pitfalls

Peeking

  • stopping when p dips below 0.05
  • seen above

Multiple testing

  • 20 metrics, 10 segments: something will "win"

Novelty effect

  • new things get clicked because they are new

Sample ratio mismatch

  • the split is off, the randomization is broken

Simpson's paradox

  • B wins in every segment and loses overall

All of them produce beautiful, confident, wrong dashboards.

Multiple testing (live)

Test 20 metrics at α = 5% and you have a 64% chance of at least one false win. Fix: one primary metric decided upfront. Everything else is exploration, not evidence.
Nothing works in this dashboard, every metric is pure noise. Slide the metric count up and watch tiles light up. "New dashboard" rolls again: a different metric wins every time, that is the point. The fix is not a stricter threshold (there are corrections for that, we skip them), it is deciding one primary metric before the test. Everything else on the dashboard is for finding ideas, not for shipping.

Novelty effect

Users click the shiny new thing, then they stop. Run long enough, look at the trend over time, and look at new users separately (they have no "old" to compare to).

Simpson's paradox

B got mostly mobile users (low conversion), A got mostly desktop users. Happens when the split differs by segment: ramp-ups per platform, or observational data. Proper randomization prevents it, segment checks detect it.
First click: only the segments. Ask: B wins on mobile and on desktop, so what does B do overall? Everyone says "wins". Second click: it loses. Let that sit, then the next slide shows why with the counts.

Simpson's paradox in numbers

mobiledesktopoverall
A (old checkout) 1,000 users × 3.0% = 30 buyers 4,000 users × 8.0% = 320 buyers 350 / 5,000 = 7.0%
B (new checkout) 4,000 users × 3.5% = 140 buyers 1,000 users × 9.0% = 90 buyers 230 / 5,000 = 4.6%
who is in the group? A: 20% of its users, B: 80% A: 80% of its users, B: 20% same size, different mix
The overall rate is a weighted average: 80% of B's users sit in the segment where almost nobody buys. "Overall" compares desktop people with mobile people, not the two checkouts. With a coin flip per user both groups get the same mix, and the winner of every segment also wins overall.
Walk through the buyers, not the rates: 30 + 320 against 140 + 90. The rates per segment favour B, the counts favour A, because the counts carry the mix. Then the last row: it is the mix that differs, not the checkout. Back to randomization: a coin flip per user gives both groups the same mix, so this cannot happen in a clean A/B test. It happens in ramp-ups per platform and whenever people compare observational data. The segment check in the SQL slide later is how you detect it.

Outlook (not exam relevant)

Bayesian A/B testing

  • answers the question people actually ask: "probability that B is better: 96%"
  • needs a prior; tools: PyMC, many platforms (VWO, Optimizely)

Sequential testing

  • peeking by design: thresholds that get stricter the more often you look
  • stops bad ideas early without inflating α

Uplift / personalization

  • "B is better for whom?"
  • a model predicts the best variant per user
  • exactly the notebook of the next lecture

Experimentation platforms

  • Optimizely, LaunchDarkly, GrowthBook (open source), in-house at the big ones

A/B tests in the data warehouse

Two tables are enough


-- who is in which group (written by the assignment service)
assignments(experiment_id, user_id, variant, assigned_at)

-- what users did (your normal fact tables)
orders(order_id, user_id, order_ts, revenue)

SELECT a.variant,
       COUNT(DISTINCT a.user_id)                AS users,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.user_id)                AS buyers,
       COUNT(DISTINCT o.user_id) * 1.0
         / COUNT(DISTINCT a.user_id)            AS conversion
FROM assignments a
LEFT JOIN orders o
       ON o.user_id = a.user_id
      AND o.order_ts >= a.assigned_at
WHERE a.experiment_id = 'checkout_v2'
GROUP BY a.variant;

Why BI people care

  • the experiment dashboard is just this query plus a confidence interval
  • metric definitions must be the same as in the company KPIs, or nobody trusts the result
  • experiment results are the most trustworthy rows in the warehouse: the only ones with a causal story
  • join with segments (device, country, new vs returning) for the Simpson check
  • SRM check = one GROUP BY

Takeaways

  • Randomize, then every difference beyond noise is causal
  • Every observed difference is effect + noise; A/A tests show how big noise is
  • The p-value: how surprising is this difference if nothing was going on?
  • α = 5% and power = 80% are conventions, the MDE is a business decision
  • Plan the sample size first, then run, then look. No peeking.
  • Report effect size with a confidence interval, not just p
  • One primary metric, guardrails, segment and SRM checks
  • Tools do the maths: statsmodels, scipy.stats
